\begin{abstract}
This report presents a statistical analysis of the Amazon Data Science Books dataset using the R programming language. The objectives are to assess data quality, describe the main characteristics of the books, and investigate how book attributes relate to price and customer ratings.

The original dataset contains 946 observations and 11 variables. During data cleaning, one exact duplicate was removed, blank strings were converted to missing values, and book weights were converted into kilograms. Potential outliers were investigated using the interquartile range (IQR) method, and ten implausible weight measurements were identified using a weight-per-page check and excluded from the weight-specific analysis. A rating consistency check flagged 90 of the 822 reviewed books (10.95\%) whose reported average rating differs from the rating implied by the star proportions; these books were retained and documented as a data quality limitation.

Descriptive statistics and visualizations show that prices, page counts and review counts are right-skewed, while average ratings are concentrated near the upper end of the five-star scale. Five hypotheses were tested using Pearson and Spearman correlation tests, Welch's t-test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Page count is positively correlated with log price ($r = 0.486$), books without reviews have a higher mean log price than books with reviews, and review volume is weakly negatively correlated with price ($\rho = -0.156$). No significant association was found between average rating and price, and the difference in ratings between high- and low-engagement books was not robust.

A multiple linear regression for log price explains about 29\% of its variation, with page count as the strongest positive predictor and review volume as a negative predictor. Diagnostic plots indicate non-normal residuals and heteroskedasticity, so the regression results should be interpreted with caution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Data analysis plays an important role in understanding patterns, identifying relationships, and extracting meaningful insights from real-world datasets. Statistical methods allow researchers to summarize large amounts of information, evaluate variability, and investigate potential associations between variables.

This report presents an exploratory statistical analysis of the Amazon Data Science Books dataset using the R programming language. The dataset contains information about books related to data science, including their prices, number of pages, average customer ratings, number of reviews, rating distributions, and physical weights.

The study has three objectives. First, to assess and improve data quality by identifying missing values, duplicate observations, inconsistent units, potential outliers and inconsistencies between rating variables. Second, to describe the distributions of the main variables using descriptive statistics and graphical methods. Third, to investigate relationships between book characteristics, prices and customer ratings using formal hypothesis tests and a multiple linear regression model.

In particular, the analysis addresses the following questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Are longer books more expensive?
    \item Do books with and without customer reviews differ in price?
    \item Do books with many reviews receive different ratings from books with few reviews?
    \item Is the number of reviews associated with price?
    \item Do higher-rated books command higher prices?
\end{itemize}

The report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the dataset and Section 3 the data cleaning process. Section 4 presents descriptive statistics and Section 5 the graphical analysis. Section 6 reports the hypothesis tests, Section 7 the regression model and its diagnostics, and Section 8 summarizes the main conclusions and limitations.

\section{Data Cleaning}
\label{sec:cleaning}
\subsection{Data Cleaning Overview}

Data cleaning was performed in R to improve data consistency while preserving as much information as possible. The procedures included handling missing values, removing duplicate observations,
standardizing weight measurements, investigating outliers,
and validating customer review information.


\subsection{Missing Values and Duplicate Observations}

Blank strings in the weight and language variables were converted to \texttt{NA}. Missing values were handled using available-case analysis rather than automatically removing incomplete observations, even when multiple values were missing from the same row.

This approach allowed observations to contribute to
analyses involving their available variables, minimizing
unnecessary information loss. However, available-case
analysis may introduce bias if missingness is systematic.

One exact duplicate was identified and removed,
reducing the dataset from 946 to 945 observations.



\subsection{Weight Standardization}

Book weights were originally recorded in two units:
ounces (204 observations) and pounds (738 observations),
with 3 missing measurements. 
The following conversion factors were applied:
\begin{align*}
\text{Ounces to kilograms:}\quad
W_{\mathrm{kg}} &= W_{\mathrm{oz}} \times 0.0283495 \\[8pt]
\text{Pounds to kilograms:}\quad
W_{\mathrm{kg}} &= W_{\mathrm{lb}} \times 0.4535924
\end{align*}

\subsection{Outlier Detection Using the IQR Method}

Potential outliers were identified using the interquartile
range (IQR) method, with boundaries defined as:
\begin{align*}
IQR &= Q_3 - Q_1 \\[6pt]
\text{Lower Bound} &= Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR \\[6pt]
\text{Upper Bound} &= Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR
\end{align*}
For book weight, the IQR was approximately 0.448 kg, producing an upper boundary of 1.602 kg. The method identified 41 potential weight outliers. Other variables also contained outliers: price (97), pages (17), average ratings (34), and review counts (111).

These observations were not automatically removed because extreme values may represent genuine book characteristics rather than recording errors.
\newpage
\subsection{Investigation of Suspicious Weight Measurements}
The IQR method identified potential weight outliers, but
these observations were not necessarily incorrect. To investigate unusual weight measurements further, the relationship between book weight and page count was examined.

A weight-per-page ratio was calculated for books with available positive page counts and weight measurements:
\[
\text{Weight per page (g/page)}
=
\frac{1000 \times W_{\mathrm{kg}}}{\text{Pages}}
\]
Measurements outside the exploratory range of
0.3--10 grams per page were flagged as potentially
suspicious. This criterion identified 10 observations.
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Criterion & Value \\
\midrule
Lower threshold & 0.3 g/page \\
Upper threshold & 10 g/page \\
Flagged observations & 10 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Criteria for Flagging Suspicious Weight Measurements}
\label{tab:weightcriteria}
\end{table} 
The flagged measurements were not confirmed data errors.
Therefore, the corresponding books were retained in the cleaned dataset. For weight-specific sensitivity analysis, a separate variable, \texttt{weight\_analysis}, was created
by replacing the 10 flagged weight values with \texttt{NA}.

The mean weight decreased from approximately 0.778 kg to
0.753 kg, while the standard deviation decreased from
0.583 kg to 0.404 kg. The Pearson correlation between
page count and weight increased from 0.571 to 0.865.
Because the filtering criterion was based on weight
relative to page count, this increase should be
interpreted cautiously rather than as independent
evidence of a stronger underlying relationship. The corresponding scatterplots are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:pagesweight}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{plots/4_6_pages_vs_weight.png}
    \caption{Relationship Between Page Count and Book Weight Before and After Filtering Suspicious Measurements}
    \label{fig:pagesweight}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pagesweight} compares the relationship
between page count and book weight before and after
excluding the ten flagged measurements from the
weight-specific sensitivity analysis.

The original standardized weight measurements include
all available values, whereas the filtered analysis
excludes potentially suspicious measurements without
removing the corresponding books from the dataset.

Although the filtered data show a stronger positive
correlation, this increase should be interpreted
cautiously because the filtering criterion itself
depends on the weight-to-page ratio.

\subsection{Review Data Consistency}

Customer review information was examined to identify
inconsistencies between review counts, average ratings,
and star-rating proportions.

A total of 123 books had zero customer reviews. For
these observations, the star-rating proportions were
set to \texttt{NA}, since no rating distribution could
be meaningfully calculated.

For the remaining 822 books with customer reviews,
the five star-rating proportions were checked to
determine whether their sum was approximately equal
to one. No observations exceeded a tolerance of
0.05 from the expected total.

An additional consistency check compared the reported
average rating with the weighted average calculated
from the star-rating proportions:
\[
R_{\mathrm{calculated}}
= 5s_5 + 4s_4 + 3s_3 + 2s_2 + s_1
\]
where $s_k$ represents the proportion of
$k$-star ratings.
The absolute difference was calculated as:
\[
D = |R_{\mathrm{reported}} - R_{\mathrm{calculated}}|
\]
Using an exploratory threshold of $D > 0.2$,
90 out of 822 books (10.95\%) were flagged
for potential rating inconsistencies.

These discrepancies were concentrated among
books with relatively few customer reviews.
Some differences exceeded what would ordinarily
be expected from rounding. However, the available
data do not confirm whether the reported average
ratings and star-rating proportions refer to
identical sets of customer evaluations.

Since the available data did not establish
which rating measure was more reliable,
the flagged observations were retained
without modification.

Although the star-rating proportions generally
summed to one, this additional check revealed
potential inconsistencies between the reported
average ratings and rating distributions.
These should be considered when interpreting
rating-related statistical analyses.

\subsection{Central Tendency and Variability}
The number of customer reviews exhibits the greatest relative
variability (CV = 362.39\%). Its mean (137.898) is considerably
higher than its median (25), indicating that a small number of
highly reviewed books influence the average.

Book prices also exhibit substantial variability (CV = 70.25\%),
while page counts and filtered weights show moderate-to-high
relative variability. In contrast, average customer ratings
have the lowest CV (9.03\%), indicating that ratings are
concentrated within a relatively narrow range.

\subsection{Distribution Shape and Skewness}

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution.
Positive values indicate a longer right tail, while
negative values indicate a longer left tail.

Customer review counts exhibit strong positive skewness
(9.467), indicating that most books receive relatively
few reviews, while a small number receive exceptionally
high counts. Book prices (2.421), page counts (1.123),
and filtered weights (1.670) are also positively skewed.

In contrast, average customer ratings are negatively
skewed (-2.011), reflecting the concentration of ratings
toward the upper end of the five-star scale.

Original book weights had a skewness of 7.209, which
decreased to 1.670 after excluding ten potentially
suspicious measurements from the weight-specific analysis.

Overall, the distributions are asymmetric, highlighting
the importance of using the median and interquartile range
alongside the mean and standard deviation.

\subsection{Log Transformation of Review Counts}

The number of customer reviews exhibited substantial
positive skewness, with a small number of books receiving
exceptionally high review counts.

To examine this distribution more clearly, a logarithmic
transformation was applied:
\[
Y = \log(1 + X)
\]
where $X$ represents the original number of reviews.
Adding one allows books with zero reviews to be included,
since $\log(0)$ is undefined.
Figure~\ref{fig:logreviews} presents the distribution
of log-transformed review counts.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{plots/4_3_log_reviews.png}
    \caption{Histogram of Log-Transformed Review Counts.}
    \label{fig:logreviews}
\end{figure}

The original review-count distribution had a skewness
of 9.467, indicating a strong right tail. Following
the transformation, skewness decreased to approximately
0.055, indicating substantially reduced asymmetry.

The transformation compresses large review counts while
preserving their relative ordering. This makes the
distribution easier to visualize and may be useful for
subsequent statistical analysis. However, low skewness
alone does not establish that the transformed data
follow a normal distribution.

\section{Hypothesis Testing and Empirical Findings}


To formally evaluate the underlying relationships and market dynamics prior to econometric modeling, five primary statistical hypotheses were formulated and empirically tested.

\subsection{Hypothesis 1: The Effect of Page Count on Book Price and Non-Parametric Modeling}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):} There is no linear correlation between the number of pages (\texttt{pages}) and the log-transformed book price ($\rho = 0$).

    \item \textbf{Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):} Page count is linearly correlated with the log-transformed book price ($\rho \neq 0$).
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{plots/hypothesis_1_price_pages.png}
    \caption{Hypothesis 1: Comparison of Parametric (OLS) and Non-Parametric (LOESS and Nadaraya--Watson) Estimators}
    \label{fig:h1_plot}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Empirical Results and Statistical Testing}
The Pearson product-moment correlation test revealed a statistically significant positive relationship between page count and $\log(1 + \text{price})$, with a correlation coefficient of $r = 0.4859$ ($t = 16.944$, $df = 929$, $p < 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$) and a 95\% confidence interval from $0.4352$ to $0.5335$. Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected.
\newpage
\subsubsection*{Graph Interpretation and Non-Parametric Benchmarking}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the region containing most observations (approximately 0--800 pages), the OLS line, the LOESS curve and the Nadaraya--Watson estimator closely agree, supporting a positive association between page count and log price.

    \item Above approximately 1{,}000 pages the data are sparse. Because the Nadaraya--Watson estimator is a local weighted average, it follows individual books in this region, producing sharp fluctuations and flat segments where no observations are nearby.

    \item The LOESS curve is smoother in this region, but its downward bend is also based on very few books. The OLS line extrapolates the linear trend into a region with almost no data.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion:} Page count is positively associated with log price across the range where most books lie. Estimates for very long books (more than 1{,}000 pages) are unreliable because of data sparsity. As this is an observational association, it does not establish that additional pages cause higher prices.

\newpage
\subsection{Hypothesis 2: Price Differentiation Based on Review Engagement}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):} The mean log-price of books without reviews (\texttt{No Reviews}) is equal to the mean log-price of books with active reviews (\texttt{With Reviews}).

    \item \textbf{Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):} There is a statistically significant difference in mean pricing between reviewed and unreviewed titles.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{plots/hypothesis_2_price_reviews.png}
    \caption{Hypothesis 2: Price Comparison by Review Engagement Status}
    \label{fig:h2_plot}
\end{figure}
\subsubsection*{Empirical Results and Group Estimates}

A Welch two-sample t-test was conducted on $\log(1 + \text{price})$ to compare the two groups without assuming equal variances. The results show a statistically significant difference in means ($t = 2.7354$, $df = 151.67$, $p = 0.006973$). The 95\% confidence interval for the difference ranges from $0.04620$ to $0.28648$, so the null hypothesis is rejected at $\alpha = 0.01$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Reviews Group:} Mean $\log(1 + \text{price}) = 3.943061$.

    \item \textbf{With Reviews Group:} Mean $\log(1 + \text{price}) = 3.776725$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation:} Books without reviews have a higher average log price than books with reviews. One possible explanation is that newly listed titles, which have not yet received reviews, are offered at their full list price, while popular titles are discounted over time. However, the dataset does not contain information on listing dates or discounts, so this explanation cannot be tested directly.

\newpage
\subsection{Hypothesis 3: Variance in Ratings Across Review Engagement Levels}

Because the average rating variable (\texttt{avg\_reviews}) is structurally missing (\texttt{NA}) for books with zero reviews, a direct comparison between unreviewed and reviewed books is unfeasible. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 evaluates rating dynamics within the subsample of reviewed books ($N = 822$), splitting them into High Engagement and Low Engagement categories based on the median review count.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):} The mean average review rating (\texttt{avg\_reviews}) does not differ significantly between books with high and low review engagement.

    \item \textbf{Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):} Books with high review engagement exhibit significantly different average ratings compared to those with low engagement.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{plots/hypothesis_3_ratings_engagement.png}
    \caption{Hypothesis 3: Average Ratings Across Review Engagement Levels}
    \label{fig:h3_plot}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Empirical Results and Methodological Conclusion}

The parametric Welch two-sample $t$-test indicates a statistically significant difference in mean ratings between engagement groups ($t = 2.008, df = 534.01, p = 0.0452$), with the High Engagement group exhibiting a slightly higher mean rating ($\bar{x} = 4.469$) compared to the Low Engagement group ($\bar{x} = 4.413$). 

However, the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test yields a non-significant result ($W = 83962, p = 0.8886$). Because the non-parametric test accounts for the severe negative skewness and extreme low-rating outliers in the distribution (particularly present in the Low Engagement subsample), we conclude that engagement volume does not exert a robust, distribution-free effect on book ratings once non-normality is controlled for.

These findings should also be interpreted in light
of the rating consistency check, which identified
potential discrepancies between reported average
ratings and star-rating proportions. No observations
were excluded solely on this basis.

\subsection{Hypothesis 4: Review Volume vs. Book Pricing Dynamics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):} There is no monotonic correlation between the number of customer reviews (\texttt{n\_reviews}) and the log-transformed book price.

    \item \textbf{Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):} There is a statistically significant monotonic correlation between review volume and book price.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{plots/hypothesis_4_reviews_price.png}
    \caption{Hypothesis 4: Relationship Between Review Volume and Book Price}
    \label{fig:h4_plot}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Empirical Results and Statistical Testing}

The Spearman rank correlation test revealed a statistically significant negative association between the number of customer reviews and log-transformed book price ($\rho = -0.1561$, $S = 161572930$, $p = 1.465 \times 10^{-6}$).

The null hypothesis is rejected, indicating a weak but statistically significant negative monotonic relationship between review volume and book price.

\newpage
\subsection{Hypothesis 5: Average Customer Rating vs. Book Price}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):} There is no monotonic correlation between the average customer rating (\texttt{avg\_reviews}) and the log-transformed book price.

    \item \textbf{Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):} There is a statistically significant monotonic correlation between average customer ratings and book price.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{plots/hypothesis_5_rating_price.png}
    \caption{Hypothesis 5: Relationship Between Average Customer Rating and Book Price}
    \label{fig:h5_plot}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Empirical Results and Statistical Testing}

The Spearman rank correlation test revealed a negligible positive association between average customer ratings and log-transformed book price ($\rho = 0.01974$, $p = 0.5723$).

Since the p-value exceeds the conventional significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$, the null hypothesis cannot be rejected. The empirical findings suggest that average customer ratings do not exhibit a statistically significant monotonic association with book price.

This result should be interpreted cautiously because
the rating consistency check identified potential
discrepancies in 90 observations. These observations
were retained in the analysis.

\section{Multivariate Econometric Modeling and Diagnostic Evaluation}

\subsection{Multiple Linear Regression Model}

To investigate the joint association between book characteristics and retail prices, a multivariate Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model was estimated. The dependent variable was the log-transformed book price, while the independent variables included page count, review availability, and log-transformed review volume.

The econometric specification is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
\log(1+\text{price}_i)
=
\beta_0
+
\beta_1\text{pages}_i
+
\beta_2\text{has\_reviews}_i
+
\beta_3\log(1+\text{n\_reviews}_i)
+
\varepsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $\beta_0$ represents the intercept, $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, and $\beta_3$ represent the regression coefficients, and $\varepsilon_i$ denotes the error term.

\subsection{Empirical Regression Results}

The estimated model was statistically significant, with an overall F-statistic of $F(3,927)=127.7$ and a p-value below $2.2 \times 10^{-16}$. The model was estimated on 931 books with complete information on all variables.

The model achieved an $R^2$ of approximately $0.2924$ and an adjusted $R^2$ of $0.2901$, indicating that approximately 29\% of the variation in log-transformed book prices is explained by the included predictors.

\midrule

\subsection{Interpretation of Regression Coefficients}
The page count coefficient is positive and highly statistically significant ($\beta_1=0.001506$, $p<0.001$). Holding the other variables constant, each additional page is associated with approximately $0.15\%$ higher price.

The review availability indicator is not statistically significant ($\beta_2=0.004104$, $p=0.946$), suggesting that the presence of reviews does not explain price differences once page count and review volume are taken into account.

Log-transformed review volume has a statistically significant negative coefficient ($\beta_3=-0.0722$, $p<0.001$). Holding page count constant, a $1\%$ increase in the number of reviews is associated with approximately $0.072\%$ lower price.

The model explains about 29\% of the variation in log price. The remaining variation is likely related to factors not available in the dataset, such as the publisher, binding type, author reputation and discounts.

\section{Conclusion}
\begingroup
\setstretch{1.0}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

This report analysed 946 Amazon data science books, of which 945 were retained after removing one duplicate. No books were removed because of extreme or missing values; missing values were handled using available-case analysis, and ten implausible weights were excluded only from the weight-specific analysis. The main findings are:

\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item \textbf{Page count is the strongest predictor of price.} It is positively correlated with log price ($r = 0.486$, $p < 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$) and remains significant in the regression, where each additional page is associated with approximately 0.15\% higher price. The relationship is uncertain for books above about 1{,}000 pages, where data are sparse.

    \item \textbf{Books with more reviews tend to be cheaper.} Books without reviews have a higher mean log price ($p = 0.007$), and review volume is weakly negatively correlated with price ($\rho = -0.156$, $p < 0.001$). In the regression, the number of reviews remains significant, while the presence of reviews does not.

    \item \textbf{Ratings are not related to price.} No significant association was found ($\rho = 0.020$, $p = 0.572$), so there is no evidence of a quality premium.

    \item \textbf{Ratings do not differ robustly by review volume.} Welch's t-test suggested a small difference ($p = 0.045$), but the Wilcoxon test did not ($p = 0.889$).

    \item \textbf{Customer ratings are predominantly positive.} On average, about 72\% of each book's ratings are five-star ratings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations and Possible Extensions}

\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item The analysis is observational, so the associations cannot be interpreted as causal effects.
    \item The rating consistency check flagged 90 books, mainly with few reviews, whose reported rating differs from the one implied by the star proportions. They were retained, which may affect rating-related results.
    \item The regression explains about 29\% of the variation in log price; factors such as publisher, binding type, author and discounts are not available.
    \item The diagnostic plots indicate non-normal residuals and heteroskedasticity, so the regression p-values should be interpreted with caution.
    \item Almost all books are in English, so the effect of language could not be studied.
\end{itemize}

Future work could use heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, include variables such as publisher or publication date, and model non-linear effects of page count on price.
